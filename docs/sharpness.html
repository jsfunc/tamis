How the sharpness score works

The small number under each thumbnail, and what it can and cannot tell you.

The short version

Every photo gets two numbers under its thumbnail. The bold one is the aesthetic quality score. The lighter one beside it is sharpness, from 0 to 100, and it answers one narrow question:

How few pixels does a real edge in this photo take to cross?

A crisp edge goes from dark to light in about one pixel. Defocus does not erase an edge — it widens it, spreading the same change over five, eight, twelve pixels. So the score is a measure of edge width: 100 means edges resolve in a single pixel, 0 means the sharpest thing found still takes eight or more.

Sort the filmstrip by sharpness with the ◎ button below the filter slider, or View → Sort by Sharpness.

What it actually measures

A window sixteen pixels long slides along every row and every column of the photo. The four pixels at each end are plateaus; the eight in the middle are where an edge would sit.

step  = mean(right plateau) − mean(left plateau)     how much it changes
width = pixels for the profile to rise from 10% to 90% of that step

That is the whole measurement. The photo's tiles are scored from the widths found inside them, and the photo's score is the average of its five sharpest tiles.

The important detail is that step is a difference of two averages, never a sum of differences. That distinction is the entire design, and the section on grain below explains why.

When does a window count as an edge?

Most of the sixteen-pixel windows in a photo contain no edge at all. Four tests decide, and each one exists because a real photo scored wrongly without it.

TestWhat it requiresWhat it removes
Amplitude The step carries at least 18 grey levels, and is at least 5× the scatter within its own plateaus. Film grain and sensor noise, with no noise model and no per-photo calibration.
Contained The profile is still near the left plateau where the window starts, and has reached the right one where it ends. Steps that already happened inside a plateau, which otherwise measure as an instant — and therefore perfect — rise.
Monotone The climb does not reverse by more than a quarter of the step. Noise that merely touches the far level on one excursion. Wandering is not an edge, however far it wanders.
No overshoot The profile approaches each plateau rather than shooting past it. Sharpening halos — the bright rim phone cameras add beside a dark region, which fakes a fast transition.

A tile needs at least 200 windows that pass all four before it will offer an opinion. One genuine edge crossing a tile lights up roughly a thousand of them, so this threshold only discards statistical flukes — a handful of chance readings in a dark, grainy patch, all of them narrow and therefore all of them wrongly flattering.

Why grain is the hard part

The obvious way to detect blur is to measure how much detail a photo contains — edge energy, or how much a deliberate re-blur changes the image. Tamis used exactly that until version 2.6, and it was wrong in a way worth explaining, because it is the trap every simple sharpness metric falls into.

Those metrics all divide one sum of differences by another. Grain is uncorrelated pixel-to-pixel noise, so it inflates such a sum without limit. Measured on synthetic test tiles through the old code:

Tile contentOld score
pure noise, σ 50.911
pure noise, σ 200.915
a perfectly sharp, noise-free edge0.800
a featureless wall0.147
the same wall with a little grain added0.809

Noise scored higher than a perfectly sharp edge, and adding grain to a blank wall took it from 0.15 to 0.81. On a real folder the old score rated a set of deliberately blurry photos above a set of good ones, and gave a sharp portrait with a soft background a score of 8 out of 100.

Averaging cancels grain instead of accumulating it. Because step is a difference of two four-pixel averages, grain shrinks it rather than inflating it — which is why the current measure reports "no edge here" for pure noise, at any amplitude, instead of reporting it as the sharpest thing in the frame.

Blank, not blurred

Some photos contain no edge the measure can read: an empty sky, a smooth wall, a subject lost in fog. These are shown with a dash (–) rather than a number, and the status bar calls them not measurable.

This is deliberate and it is not the same as scoring zero. Zero means "measured, and out of focus". A dash means the photo never offered evidence either way — a perfectly sharp photograph of a blank wall looks identical to a blurred one at this level. Sorting by sharpness puts these photos at the end alongside the not-yet-scored, rather than sinking them below genuinely soft frames.

What the score cannot tell you

The score is the average of the five sharpest tiles anywhere in the frame, and nothing ties those tiles to your subject.

A motion-blurred portrait taken in front of a sunlit window scores high — on the window. Venetian blinds, a wall's architectural lines, a hard shadow boundary: all genuinely sharp, all quite capable of speaking for a photo whose actual subject is smeared. The measurement is honest; the question it answers is simply narrower than "is this a good photo".

So read a high score as "something in this frame is in focus", and a low one as "worth a look". It is a tool for finding candidates to examine, not a verdict to cull by.

Two related limits worth knowing:

  • Depth of field is not a defect. A portrait shot wide open is mostly out-of-focus background. The score reports on the sharp part, which is the intent — but it means the number describes the subject only when the subject is the sharpest thing present.
  • Focus can be where you did not want it. If a portrait's chest is sharp and the face is soft, the score reports the chest, correctly and unhelpfully.

Resolution and cost

Edge width is counted in pixels, so the resolution the photo is measured at is part of the measure. Tamis decodes each photo at half its stored size — four times cheaper than full resolution, and on a 28-photo trial it reordered almost nothing (rank correlation 0.955).

Quarter resolution was tested and rejected. The whole span from sharp to fully defocused compresses into about one usable pixel there, and the ranking degrades badly: leaving the scale alone made a completely defocused photo score 84, and rescaling it pinned six photos — including a good one — at exactly zero.

The measure costs roughly 0.6 seconds per photo, which is most of the time the background scoring pass spends. It runs once per photo and is cached in .tamis_quality.json beside your images, so a folder is only ever scored once. Deleting that file forces a rescore.

Reading the number in practice

ScoreUsually means
90–100Edges resolve in about a pixel. Something here is properly in focus.
70–90Sharp enough for most uses; fine detail may be slightly soft.
40–70Visibly soft. Worth opening at full size before keeping.
0–40Nothing in the frame resolves well. Usually camera shake or missed focus.
–No edge to measure. The score has no opinion, in either direction.

The numbers are only meaningful relative to each other, and only within a comparable set of photos. Different cameras, lenses and processing shift the whole scale, so compare within a shoot rather than across your library.
